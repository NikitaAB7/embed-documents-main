QUESTION: What are the mandatory conditions for a close-ended scheme to avoid listing on stock exchanges?

- The scheme opens for repurchase within a period of six months from the closure of subscription.

These exceptions are as per SEBI regulations and allow a close-ended scheme to avoid mandatory listing on stock exchanges if any of the above conditions are fulfilled[C2].

5. The asset allocation must be as per the Scheme Information Document, and all investments must comply with the restrictions specified in the SEBI (Mutual Funds) Regulations, 1996, and applicable circulars [C2][C8].

These restrictions are designed to ensure that Gold ETF schemes remain true to their investment objectives and maintain adequate liquidity.

QUESTION: What are the specific restrictions for Capital Protection Oriented Schemes?

ANSWER:
The specific restrictions for Capital Protection Oriented Schemes are as follows:

1. **Portfolio Structure and Rating**: The orientation towards capital protection must originate from the portfolio structure of the scheme, not from any bank guarantee, insurance cover, etc. The proposed portfolio structure must be rated by a SEBI-registered Credit Rating Agency to assess the degree of certainty for achieving capital protection. 2. **Continuous Monitoring**: Asset Management Companies (AMCs) must ensure continuous monitoring of the portfolio structure and quarterly review of the ratings. These disclosures and reviews must be confirmed in the offer documents [C5].

3. **Regulatory Compliance**: All investment restrictions as contained in the relevant SEBI Mutual Fund Regulations and applicable schedules must be incorporated. If the fund follows any additional internal norms (such as limiting exposure to a particular security or sector), these must also be disclosed [C6].

4. **No External Guarantees**: The scheme cannot rely on any external guarantees (such as bank guarantees or insurance covers) for capital protection; the protection must be inherent to the portfolio structure [C2].

5. **Credit Rating Requirement**: The units of the scheme must be rated by a registered credit rating agency from the viewpoint of the ability of its portfolio structure to attain protection of the capital invested [C1].

In summary, Capital Protection Oriented Schemes must rely solely on their portfolio structure for capital protection, require regular credit rating and monitoring, and must comply with all SEBI investment restrictions and disclosure requirements. No external guarantees are permitted [C1][C2][C5][C6].

- The criteria also extend to trustees and directors, who must not have been found guilty of moral turpitude or convicted of economic offences [C8].

In summary, the 'Fit and Proper Person' criteria is a foundational standard that interacts with and is referenced by multiple SEBI regulations, ensuring that only individuals and entities with integrity, sound reputation, and clean legal records are permitted to participate in the mutual fund industry [C1][C2][C3][C5][C7][C8].

QUESTION: Explain the complete shareholding web restriction under Regulation 7B and its exception provisions.

ANSWER:
The "complete shareholding web restriction" under Regulation 7B generally restricts cross-holdings and significant shareholding overlaps between sponsors, asset management companies (AMCs), and mutual funds to prevent conflicts of interest and ensure independence in the mutual fund industry.

**Exception Provisions:**
However, several exceptions to these restrictions are provided, as outlined below:

1. **Dual Registration for Sponsors:**  
   A sponsor is allowed to obtain both a regular mutual fund registration and a separate registration as a "Mutual Fund Lite" (a lighter regulatory regime for certain passive schemes), subject to conditions specified by SEBI, even if Regulation 7B would otherwise prohibit such dual roles [C1].

2. **Transfer of Passive Schemes:**  
   An existing sponsor may transfer its eligible passive schemes (those qualifying as mutual fund lite schemes) from its existing mutual fund to a mutual fund lite belonging to a group entity of the same sponsor, again subject to SEBI's conditions. However, after such a transfer, the original mutual fund cannot launch new passive schemes eligible as mutual fund lite schemes [C1].

3. **Shareholding in Group Entities:**  
   An existing shareholder who holds 10% or more shareholding or voting rights in an existing AMC of a mutual fund may also be allowed to hold 10% or more in a mutual fund lite AMC belonging to a group entity of the same sponsor, notwithstanding the general restriction under Regulation 7B [C1].

**Summary:**  
Regulation 7B imposes restrictions to prevent complex or overlapping shareholding structures among mutual fund sponsors and AMCs. However, exceptions allow for dual registrations, transfer of passive schemes, and certain cross-shareholdings within group entities, provided SEBI's specified conditions are met [C1].

QUESTION: What is the complete process and implications when institutional mechanism detects potential front-running?

ANSWER:
When an institutional mechanism detects potential front-running, the process and implications are as follows:

1. Detection and Identification:
- Asset Management Companies (AMCs) are required to have an institutional mechanism in place for the identification and deterrence of potential market abuse, including front-running and fraudulent transactions in securities, as specified by SEBI regulations[C1].
- This involves integrated systems (front-mid-back office) that perform order generation, pre-trade compliance checks, order execution, deal booking, and exception reporting. These systems are designed to flag breaches, such as unauthorized trades or trades that violate preset parameters[C3].
- Data analytics and Management Information Systems (MIS) are used to identify fraud patterns and generate alerts for potential front-running incidents[C5].

2. Internal Review and Reporting:
- When alerts are generated, the compliance officer reviews the details and investigates the incident to determine if front-running or other violations have occurred[C5].
- The compliance officer is responsible for tracking employee transactions and mutual fund transactions to ensure there is no conflict of interest or front-running by employees[C4].
- A report containing details of the alerts generated and the subsequent actions taken is submitted to the trustees on a quarterly basis[C6].

3. Escalation and External Reporting:
- Trustees review the reports, add their comments and details of steps taken, and may forward the results to SEBI in their half-yearly trustee reports[C6].

4. Implications:
- If front-running is confirmed, it may lead to disciplinary action against the involved employees, including negative impact on performance incentives, clawback of incentives, or other penalties as per the AMC’s policies and SEBI regulations[C7].
- The AMC must document the incident, update fraud risk registers, and may need to enhance training or controls to prevent recurrence[C5].
- There may also be regulatory consequences, including further investigation or penalties imposed by SEBI if the matter is escalated[C6].

In summary, the process involves detection through automated systems, internal review by compliance officers, reporting to trustees, and possible escalation to SEBI, with implications ranging from internal disciplinary action to regulatory penalties[C1][C3][C4][C5][C6][C7].

QUESTION: How does the networth maintenance requirement interact with sponsor disassociation provisions?

ANSWER:
The networth maintenance requirement and sponsor disassociation provisions are closely linked in the SEBI Mutual Fund regulatory framework:

1. **Networth Requirement for AMCs**: Asset Management Companies (AMCs) must maintain a minimum networth as specified by SEBI regulations (typically INR 50 crore or INR 100 crore, depending on circumstances) on a continuous basis. This is to ensure financial stability and investor protection, especially if the sponsor does not meet certain eligibility criteria at the time of application or during operations [C1][C6].

2. **Sponsor Disassociation**: SEBI allows a sponsor to voluntarily reduce its stake and eventually disassociate from the AMC, subject to certain conditions and timelines (e.g., 5 years for listed AMCs, 3 years for unlisted AMCs). During the transition period, the outgoing sponsor must continue to fulfill all regulatory obligations applicable to sponsors [C2][C5].

3. **Interaction Between Networth and Disassociation**:
   - When a sponsor disassociates, the AMC may become "self-sponsored." In this scenario, the AMC is required to maintain the minimum networth requirement on a continuous basis as per SEBI MF Regulations [C2].
   - If the AMC fails to meet the eligibility criteria for a self-sponsored AMC (including the networth requirement), a cure period of one year is provided. If the AMC still fails after this period, either the disassociated sponsor or a new entity must step in as the sponsor of the Mutual Fund [C4][C8].
   - The statutory auditor must certify compliance with the networth and other eligibility criteria annually, and the AMC/trustees must report compliance status to SEBI regularly [C2].

4. **Summary of Interaction**: The networth maintenance requirement acts as a safeguard during and after sponsor disassociation. It ensures that, even in the absence of a traditional sponsor, the AMC remains financially robust. If the AMC cannot maintain the required networth post-disassociation, regulatory provisions require the re-association of a sponsor to protect investors and maintain regulatory compliance [C2][C4][C8].

**In essence:** The networth maintenance requirement is a critical condition for AMCs, especially when a sponsor disassociates. Failure to maintain networth can trigger the need for a new or returning sponsor, ensuring the mutual fund's stability and compliance with SEBI regulations.

QUESTION: What is the complete framework for inter-scheme transfers and their regulatory treatment?

ANSWER:
The complete framework for inter-scheme transfers (ISTs) and their regulatory treatment is as follows:

1. **Permissibility and Pricing**:
   - Transfers of investments from one scheme to another within the same mutual fund are allowed only if such transfers are done at the prevailing market price for quoted instruments on a spot basis. This ensures fair valuation and transparency in the transaction [C1][C7].

2. **Conformity with Investment Objectives**:
   - The securities transferred must be in conformity with the investment objective of the scheme to which the transfer is made [C7].

3. **Scheme Type Specific Rules**:
   - **Close Ended Schemes**: IST purchases are allowed only during the New Fund Offer (NFO) period. After the NFO, no ISTs are permitted to or from close ended schemes [C7].
   - **Open Ended Schemes**: ISTs are allowed in specific scenarios, such as for meeting liquidity requirements in a scheme in case of unanticipated redemption pressure [C7].

4. **Documentation and Disclosure**:
   - Details of ISTs during the quarter, including dates, names of transferor and transferee schemes, securities transferred and price, value of transfer, and specific reasons for the transfer, must be documented and disclosed [C4].

5. **Compliance and Safeguards**:
   - Mutual funds must comply with SEBI guidelines on ISTs, including those outlined in SEBI Circulars (e.g., SEBI/HO/IMD/DF4/CIR/P/2020/202 dated October 08, 2020, and SEBI/HO/IMD/DF2/CIR/P/2020/253 dated December 31, 2020) [C6][C7].
   - The risk management framework of the mutual fund must ensure compliance with regulatory requirements and protection of investors’ interests [C8].

6. **Legal and Regulatory Framework**:
   - ISTs are governed by the Securities and Exchange Board of India (Mutual Funds) Regulations, 1996, and amendments thereto, as well as the Indian Trusts Act, 1882 [C1].

In summary, ISTs are tightly regulated to ensure fair pricing, alignment with scheme objectives, transparency, and investor protection, with specific rules for open and close ended schemes and mandatory disclosures and compliance requirements [C1][C4][C6][C7][C8].

QUESTION: What are the complete valuation requirements when a debt security is downgraded below investment grade?

ANSWER:
When a debt security is downgraded below investment grade, the complete valuation requirements are as follows:

1. **Valuation at Price Provided by Valuation Agencies:** All money market and debt securities rated below investment grade must be valued at the price provided by valuation agencies. If the AMC deviates from this price, it must record a detailed rationale for the deviation, including information about the security (ISIN, issuer name, rating, etc.), the price at which the security was valued versus the price from the valuation agencies, and the impact of such deviation on the scheme NAV (in amount and percentage terms). This information must be reported to the Board of AMC and Trustees [C2].

2. **Use of Indicative Haircuts:** Until the valuation agencies compute the valuation of such securities, they should be valued based on indicative haircuts provided by these agencies. These haircuts are to be applied on the date of the credit event (i.e., when the security is downgraded to sub-investment grade) [C2].

3. **Treatment of Accrued Interest:** The treatment of accrued interest and future accrual of interest for securities classified as below investment grade or default is governed by SEBI guidelines, as referenced in SEBI Circular No. SEBI/HO/IMD/DF4/CIR/P/2019/102 dated September 24, 2019 [C5].

4. **Disclosure and Reporting:** The AMC must disclose details of investments downgraded to below investment grade or default during the quarter, including comments on steps taken or to be taken by the AMC [C3][C8]. Additionally, the aggregate market value and provision thereof for such securities must be disclosed [C6].

5. **Rebalancing Requirement:** If the security is part of a scheme based on a debt index and is downgraded below the rating mandated in the index methodology (including below investment grade), the portfolio must be rebalanced within 30 calendar days. This does not apply to sectoral or thematic debt indices [C1].

In summary, the valuation must follow the price from valuation agencies (or indicative haircuts if not available), with detailed documentation and reporting of any deviations, proper treatment of accrued interest, and required disclosures and rebalancing as per SEBI guidelines.

QUESTION: How does the 'associate' definition vary across different contexts and what are the implications?

ANSWER:
The definition of "associate" varies across different regulatory and operational contexts, particularly in the mutual fund industry, and these variations have important implications for disclosure, governance, and conflict of interest management.

**Regulatory Definition and Exemptions:**
- The SEBI regulations define an "associate" in relation to an asset management company (AMC), trustee, or sponsor as:
  - (i) Any person who, directly or indirectly (alone or with relatives), exercises control over the AMC, trustee, or sponsor;
  - (ii) Any person over whom the AMC, trustee, or sponsor exercises control;
  - (iii) Any entity whose director, officer, or employee is also a director, officer, or employee of the AMC (excluding independent directors)[C2].
- However, there is a specific exemption: this definition does not apply to sponsors that invest in various companies on behalf of beneficiaries of insurance policies or other schemes as specified by SEBI[C1][C2]. This means that certain institutional investors acting in a fiduciary capacity are not considered "associates" for regulatory purposes, reducing the compliance burden and potential conflict-of-interest disclosures in such cases.

**Operational and Disclosure Implications:**
- In practice, mutual funds must disclose transactions with associates, including:
  - Underwriting obligations for issues of associate companies,
  - Investments in issues lead-managed by associates,
  - Business given to associate brokers and commissions paid,
  - Distribution of units by associate companies,
  - Policies for investing in group companies, and
  - Names of associates with whom the fund has dealings or uses for marketing/distribution[C4][C5].
- If a scheme invests more than 25% of its net assets in group companies, this must be specifically disclosed[C4].

**Implications:**
- The variation in the definition of "associate" affects which entities and transactions are subject to enhanced disclosure and regulatory scrutiny.
- Exempting certain sponsors (like insurance companies investing on behalf of policyholders) from the "associate" definition reduces administrative complexity and recognizes the distinct fiduciary role of such sponsors[C1][C2].
- For other entities, the broad definition ensures transparency and helps manage conflicts of interest, as all significant relationships and transactions with associates must be disclosed and justified to investors and regulators[C4][C5].

In summary, the definition of "associate" is context-dependent, with regulatory carve-outs for certain fiduciary sponsors, and has significant implications for disclosure, governance, and conflict-of-interest management in mutual funds.

- Only a mutual fund registered under regulation 9 may be granted approval to establish a Specialized Investment Fund, subject to eligibility criteria and in the manner specified by SEBI (the Board) [C4].
- Investments made must comply with investment restrictions as specified under regulation 49AA [C4].

**2. Procedure for Launching Investment Strategies:**
- Investment strategies under the SIF must be launched in accordance with the procedure applicable to mutual fund schemes as laid down in regulation 28, unless otherwise specified by SEBI [C1].
- Each investment strategy can be structured as open-ended, close-ended, or interval, with the subscription and redemption frequency clearly disclosed in the offer document [C1].
- Fees and expenses for each strategy must comply with regulation 52 [C1].

**3. Investment Restrictions:**
- No more than 20% of the NAV of an investment strategy may be invested in debt instruments (money market and non-money market) from a single issuer, rated not below investment grade. This can be extended to 25% with prior approval from the Board of Trustees and the AMC Board [C3].
- No investment strategy may invest more than 10% of its NAV in equity shares and equity-related instruments of any entity [C6].
- The SIF, across all its strategies, must not own more than 10% of the capital carrying voting rights or units of REITs issued by a single issuer [C6].
- For InvITs, the SIF (across all strategies) must not own more than 20% of units issued by a single issuer, and no strategy may invest more than 20% of its NAV in InvITs or more than 10% in units of a single InvIT issuer, except for index or sector/industry-specific schemes [C6].

- Portfolio disclosures must be made as specified by SEBI [C6].
- The Scheme Information Document must include information about the investment approach, risk control, whether the strategy is active or passive, asset allocation, portfolio turnover policy, and the probable effects of the strategy on portfolio turnover and costs [C7].

**5. Fund Manager Requirements:**
- The Fund Manager must have the relevant NISM certification as specified by SEBI [C3].

**6. General Compliance:**
- All other provisions of the SEBI Mutual Fund Regulations, guidelines, and circulars apply to SIFs and their investment strategies, unless specifically provided otherwise [C5][C8].

**Summary of Process:**
1. Obtain SEBI approval to establish a SIF (must be a registered mutual fund).
2. Launch investment strategies following mutual fund scheme procedures, with required disclosures and compliance with investment restrictions.
3. Ensure offer documents and ongoing disclosures meet SEBI requirements.

QUESTION: What is the scheme's cash and cash equivalent position? Is the scheme fully invested or holding excess liquidity, and what are the implications?

ANSWER:
Based on the provided contexts, the cash and cash equivalent position of a scheme depends on its category:

- For open-ended debt schemes (except Overnight Fund, Liquid Fund, Gilt Fund, and Gilt Fund with 10-year constant duration), the scheme must hold at least 20% of its net assets in liquid assets such as securities, T-bills, and Repo on Government Securities[C1].
- For Liquid Funds, there is also a requirement to hold at least 20% of net assets in liquid assets. If the exposure falls below this threshold, the AMC must restore compliance before making further investments[C2].

Whether the scheme is fully invested or holding excess liquidity depends on its adherence to these regulatory minimums. If a scheme holds only the minimum required (20%), it is close to being fully invested. If it holds more than 20%, it is holding excess liquidity.

Implications:
- Holding the minimum required liquidity allows the scheme to maximize returns by investing more in higher-yielding assets, but may increase liquidity risk.
- Holding excess liquidity can reduce returns (since liquid assets typically yield less) but improves the scheme’s ability to meet redemption requests and manage risk.

In summary, the scheme must at least meet the 20% liquidity requirement, and whether it is fully invested or holding excess liquidity will impact its risk-return profile as described above[C1][C2].
